when they are free

Eight sketches for the date window — the control the Vision calls the constraint nobody plans around, and the M1 doc calls the highest-value control in the product. Prior art, the psychology behind it, the journey it sits in, and eight ways it could go.

01

Why this control gets the eight minutes

A Crazy 8s is worthless aimed at a vague area. The docs already argue this one is the sharpest target in the product — three independent lines converge on it.

  • The Vision makes it a value. Value 2 is dates are the constraint nobody plans around, bought at the cost of place-first search: "the question is not where is a dark campsite, it is which campsite, on which specific nights."
  • M1 makes it the thesis slice. Slice 2 is the one that "carries the product thesis," and the date control is named "the highest-value control in the product" because it inverts the category's question — instead of is this campground free on these nights, the user says when they are free and the whole destination answers back.
  • The research says the pattern is unclaimed. Nobody combines a scarcity-encoded calendar (Hopper) with a site-level grid (recreation.gov). Every incumbent owns one half. That gap is exactly where this control sits.
The brief in one line. Ask a person when they are free, and answer with what is open across an entire destination — honestly enough that they trust it, and fast enough that they act before the opening is gone.

Constraints carried in from the docs. Reservable, first-come and seasonally closed stay three distinct answers, worded rather than colour-coded. Availability colour is sacred data language: green reservable, blue first-come, grey taken. Freshness is stamped. Nothing may claim a number we cannot observe. Every sketch below obeys these; where one strains them, it says so.

03

What we know about the person using it

Six camper types, one split that matters more than the six. The date control has to serve both sides of it without becoming two controls.

The split: satisficers and maximisers

Schwartz's distinction does more work here than any persona. A satisficer wants a result that clears the bar and stops looking. A maximiser wants the best one and will keep looking until certain. Our six camper types sort cleanly, and they want opposite things from the same control.

ModeCamper typesWhat they type firstWhat the control owes them
SatisficerThe flexible weekender · First-timers · Off-season travellersA time, not a place — "some weekend soon"A shortlist of windows that work, ranked, with the decision already mostly made. Showing them thirty days is a cost, not a service.
MaximiserThe determined planner · RV & trailer · Families with young kidsA specific place, often a specific site or loopConfirmation and precision: exactly which nights, which sites, and proof the fit constraints hold. Summarising is a cost — they want the grid.

Six principles the sketches are tested against

  • Hick's law. Thirty days × nine campgrounds is not a choice, it is a stall. Anything that presents the raw cross-product loses to anything that pre-narrows.
  • Recognition over recall. Nobody should have to remember which campground had which nights. If comparing requires memory, the control has failed — this is the single strongest argument against per-campground calendars, which is what the whole category ships.
  • Loss aversion, handled carefully. "2 of 4 nights open" moves people more than "2 available" — scarcity is genuinely motivating, and genuinely true here. But manufactured urgency is the category's sin, and our sixth value forbids it. Scarcity may be reported, never performed.
  • Anchoring. The first window shown sets expectations for the whole session. Defaulting to today anchors a planning product to the one window that is almost never bookable; defaulting to the next window that actually works anchors to a win.
  • The Zeigarnik effect. An unfinished task keeps its grip. An alert is a deliberate open loop — which is why setting one feels good rather than like a defeat, and why the empty state should offer it rather than apologise.
  • Peak–end. The peak of planning is the moment a window turns out to work. The control's job is to manufacture that moment early and let it be legible; the end is the hand-off, which must not feel like being dumped on a government website.
The trap the whole category falls into. Every competitor optimises for the maximiser, because the maximiser is the one who knows what to type. The satisficer — over half the market, per the 56% who could not book at all — is left typing campground names they have no reason to know. The eight sketches deliberately over-index on that half.
04

The journey, and where each camper enters it

Six stages from itch to group chat. Nobody walks all six in order — what distinguishes the camper types is which stage they enter at, and which stage is their gate.

01

The itch

"We should get out before it gets cold." No park, no dates, just an impulse and a rough season.

Enters here: the flexible weekender
02

A place

A name gets typed. Today it returns a parish in Louisiana; after M1a-1 it returns a park with nine campgrounds under it.

Enters here: first-timers
03 · the sprint target

A window

When am I free, and what is open then — across the whole destination at once. Every other stage inherits the answer.

Gate for: weekender, off-season
04

The fit

Will the rig fit, is there water, how far is it from what we came for. Availability says can; this says should.

Gate for: RV & trailer, families
05

Commit

Hand off to the operator — or, when nothing is open, set an alert from the screen already in front of you.

Enters here: the determined planner
06

Bring the group

One link carrying destination, window and filters. The plan travels without a screenshot.

Everyone, eventually

Two things fall out of drawing it this way. First, stage 3 is the only stage every camper type passes through, which is what justifies spending the sprint on it. Second, the two stages with the most entrants — 1 and 5 — are the two the product currently handles worst: the itch has nowhere to go without a park name, and committing means meeting a numeric ID.

Where the feature set already lands. Stage 2 is M1a-1/2. Stage 3 is M1a-9, sketched below. Stage 4 is M1a-6 and M1a-17. Stage 5 is Slice 4 — alerts in context, which the docs say could ship beside M0 and would be the first thing a user notices. Stage 6 is the URL contract: dates in the link, decided. Stage 1 is the one with no owner in M1a at all — it belongs to M3 discovery, and sketch 6 below is the cheapest bridge to it.

06

The dot vote, and what I'd build

Crazy 8s ends in a heat-map vote, then one solution sketch. Here is the recommendation going in — argue with it rather than starting from nothing.

Build · the base

02 · The two-segment bar

The only sketch that keeps reservable, first-come and taken as three answers, which the data-language value makes non-negotiable. It is already the spec'd design; the sprint's job was to test whether anything beat it, and nothing did on that constraint.

Build · the entry

06 · The weekend shelf

Graft it on as the empty and no-dates state. It costs one component, serves the half of the market nobody serves, and it is the only thing in M1a that gives stage 1 — the itch — somewhere to go.

Prototype next

04 · The season ribbon

Answers "when should I go at all," which the calendar structurally cannot. Cheap as a header strip above the month grid. Test it after the base lands.

Hold for M2

07 · The sentence

This is hyper search with a date picker's face. Do not build it now — but let the token vocabulary here inform the verbs M2 will need, since M1a is what earns the right to ask that question.

What the eight minutes actually settled

  • The spec'd design survives contact. Panel 2 was not the most interesting sketch, and it won anyway — because the three-status rule eliminates every pure-colour approach. That is a value doing its job.
  • The satisficer has no front door. Five of eight panels only work once a destination is chosen. Panel 6 is the exception, and it exposes a real gap: stage 1 of the journey has no owner in M1a.
  • Length of stay is an input we throw away. Panels 3 and 5 both treat nights as a variable; the current model treats it as a by-product of two clicks. Worth carrying into the M1a-9 spec regardless of which shape wins.
  • Two sketches are really other milestones. Panel 7 is M2, panel 8 leans on caching Will has not built yet. Naming that early is the point of sketching badly and fast.
To run this for real: eight minutes, one panel a minute, no editing between — quantity is the mechanism, and the bad ones do necessary work. Then everyone gets three dots, silent voting, no pitching first. Winner goes to a solution sketch at real device pixels against the numbers in M1 section 8: 300×38 control, 392px popover, 432px sidebar.
