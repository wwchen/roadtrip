Roadtrip · Research

Five campers, and what stops them

Evidence-grounded personas to replace the six inferred ones in the Vision. Three we build for, two we design around — and an honest account of the time, money and access barriers that decide who gets to camp at all.

01

What changed since the Vision

The Vision named six camper types and flagged them, correctly, as desk research: "the six campers are inferred, not observed, and nobody has watched a real person plan a trip." These five are still not interview-derived — but every one is now anchored to published participation, barrier and behavioural data rather than to intuition.

The one finding that should reorder the roadmap. The industry's problem is not acquisition, it is retention — and the gap between "43% of first experiences are great" and "87% of great first experiences convert" is the entire opportunity. A planning product cannot make a campground nicer. It can make sure nobody arrives at the wrong one, on the wrong nights, without water, forty minutes from what they came for.

Is this “Google Flights for camping”?

Half of it, and the half it gets right is worth being precise about. Google Flights' real power is not search — it is the flexible-date grid. It reframes the question from “is this specific flight available” to “when is this trip possible, and what does moving a day cost me.” That is exactly the reframe M1a-9 makes, for exactly the reason: two of our three key personas hold dates fixed and treat destination as negotiable, and every camping product in the market asks for the destination first.

But one mechanic does not transfer, and getting it wrong would be expensive. Flights are fungible — a seat is a seat, so ranking by price is honest. Campsites are not. Site 43 backing onto the trail and site 12 on the gravel loop are the same price and a completely different trip. Any ranking that implies “cheapest wins” imports Google Flights' logic into a category where it is false, and produces exactly the arrival our retention data says we cannot afford.

So the first-timer's question is two questions, and only one of them is Google Flights.
“Is this weekend possible at all, anywhere near me?” — the date-window half. Answered by M1a-9, the weekend shelf, and letting a search run with no destination. Nobody in the category answers this today.
“Will I actually be OK when I get there?” — the half with no Google Flights equivalent, and the half where a first-timer is genuinely lost. Answered by stated absences, conditions, statuses as words, and a one-line reason to stay. This is where the 43%→87% gap lives. Search failure costs a trip; fit failure costs a camper, and the doc's evidence says they blame themselves and don't come back.

The practical consequence for a person who has never camped: the honest recommendation is often the less adventurous campground. 56% of new campers now prefer locations with a full range of amenities — restrooms, on-site staff, running water — and 87% of those who have a great first trip continue. Surfacing that clearly is not dumbing the product down; it is the highest-leverage retention move available to us, and it costs one line of copy per place page.

Free, flexible, or committed — which reservation are we in?

All of them, and the POV is that we are the layer before the reservation, not the reservation itself. Camping inventory sits on a commitment ladder, and every competitor in the category shows one or two rungs of it. Showing the whole ladder honestly — including the rungs with no reservation at all — is the position nobody occupies.

RungWhat it commits you toWho it's right for
Free · dispersed & first-comeNothing. No booking exists, so no cancellation, no fee, no guarantee. The risk is a “FULL” sign after a two-hour drive.The Weekender. She is structurally a fallback user of every public campground in America, and this tier is the only one still open when she looks.
Flexible · family sitesBarely a commitment — a 1-day cancellation cliff and a small fee. This is an option, not a booking.Everyone. The default, and the rung the whole category already serves well.
Committed · group facilitiesReal money at risk: a 14-day cancellation cliff, enforced minimum occupancy, headcount-banded pricing.The Organizer and the Quartermaster — and it is precisely the wrong shape for both of them.
Locked · permits & lotteriesYou commit before you know whether you're in.The Loyalist. Out of M1a scope.
The trap this framing exposes: free is not a beginner feature. It reads like one — no money, no booking window, nothing to lose. But the first-timer's failure mode is arriving somewhere wrong, and a first-come site is the single most reliable way to arrive at a “FULL” sign with a car full of borrowed gear. That is the exact experience that ends a camping career, and our own data says only 43% of first trips are rated great while 87% of the great ones convert. Surface first-come hardest to the Weekender, and softly — with the risk stated in words — to the Beginner. Same inventory, opposite recommendation, and it turns on which persona is looking.

And the Organizer's bind is that the two commitment curves run opposite. Group inventory demands its firmest commitment — the 14-day cliff, the enforced minimum — at exactly the moment her headcount is least settled, because a free yes converts to attendance only about three times in four. We cannot fix her people. What we can do is show her the rungs that tolerate uncertainty, and make “yes” cheap to give: a link that carries the dates, the filters and the live availability, so agreeing costs a glance instead of a meeting.

The analogies, audited

Three have been proposed. Each one transfers a mechanic and imports a falsehood, so it is worth writing down which half we are taking.

AnalogyWhat transfersWhat breaks
Google FlightsThe flexible-date grid. “When is this possible” instead of “is this specific thing available.” This is M1a-9.Fungibility. A seat is a seat; site 43 and site 12 are the same price and a different trip, so “cheapest wins” is a lie here.
GametimeThe timing instinct, and it is the closest of the three. Perishable inventory, supply reappearing as the date approaches, a user who wins by waiting.The engine. Gametime runs on price — resale, dynamic pricing, sellers dumping before kickoff. Camping has no resale market and no dynamic pricing: a $25 site is $25 six months out and at 4pm. Resy / OpenTableNotify-me on a released table, and the idea that the reservation is someone else's system.Cadence. A restaurant is a Tuesday decision; a campground is a trip with a drive, gear and other people attached.
Gametime's mechanic with a different payoff: availability, not discount. Supply does reappear near the date — not because prices fall, but because family sites carry a 1-day cancellation cliff, so held inventory dumps back into the pool in the last 24–48 hours. That is a real, exploitable dynamic and it is what an alert is for. Add the one source Gametime has no analogue for — first-come inventory, which is 100% allocated on the day by definition — and the last-minute pool is genuinely large. We just never get to call it a bargain.

But we are a short-horizon product, not a last-minute one, and the distinction decides the UI. Gametime optimises the final 48 hours. Our evidence says the crunch runs from about five weeks out to the morning of: 54% of campers book within five weeks, and the Weekender is deciding on Tuesday for Saturday. A five-week band wants a date window and a standing alert. A 48-hour band wants a “tonight” feed. We should ship the first and keep the second as one shelf inside it — a weekend/tonight view showing only what is claimable right now.

And there is a reason not to lean all the way in. Gametime's model teaches users to wait. That is fine when waiting costs a worse seat; it is not fine when waiting costs a first-timer a two-hour drive to a “FULL” sign, which is the failure this document says ends camping careers. Last-minute is a capability we offer the Weekender, not a habit we should train in the Beginner.

Still missing, stated plainly. No user interviews and no usage data, exactly as the Vision said. Five interviews would still test more of this page than another month of desk research. What has changed is that the guesses now have numbers attached, and several of the original six turned out to be the same person seen from different angles.

02

The model underneath

Leisure research has a standard frame for "why people who want to, don't" — Crawford & Godbey's hierarchy of constraints. It matters here because it predicts which barriers software can touch.

ConstraintWhat it isCan a planning product help?
Intrapersonal"I'm not the kind of person who does this." Competence, belonging, self-efficacy. Encountered first and hardest.Partly — by removing the moments that confirm the fear
InterpersonalNo one to go with; can't align a group. The industry's own barrier list names "a participation companion."Partly — sharing and coordination
StructuralMoney, time, transport, reservation systems. The last barrier encountered, not the first.Yes, for booking and knowledge. No, for PTO or a car.
The uncomfortable implication. Every existing camping tool — ours included — solves structural friction. That means we serve people who already cleared the intrapersonal gate. The constraint ordering "denotes a hierarchy of social privilege," and our product sits at the top of it. Persona 2 exists specifically to push against that.

One more split does more work than the six types did: satisficers versus maximisers. A satisficer wants a result that clears the bar and to stop looking. A maximiser wants the best one and keeps looking. They want opposite things from the same date control, and the whole category has optimised for the maximiser — because the maximiser is the one who knows what to type.

03

Three we build for

Ranked by how much of the product's value they capture, not by how loud they are.

Key · 01Satisficer~42% casual segment

The Flexible Weekender

Commits early, plans late. Where is negotiable; when is not.

It's Tuesday. There's a weekend coming and no plan. She's not choosing between Acadia and Olympic — she's choosing between going somewhere and not going. She has two days, a car, and about forty minutes of patience before this becomes next weekend's problem.

Time

Two days, not five. 62% of campers say weekends dominate their calendar, and 63% stay within 150 miles of home. The ideal road trip Americans describe — 13 hours of driving over five days — is a different product.

Money

Not the blocker. Fuel and a $25 site. But she'll abandon rather than pay to find out, which is why every paid alert service loses her.

Opportunity

The killer. 54% of campers book within five weeks; the good inventory was allocated 180 days out in a sixty-second auction. She is, structurally, a fallback user of every public campground in America.
EvidenceKOA calls this behaviour "SnapCamping." 40% of US hotel bookings now happen within seven days; 29% of all bookings within two weeks. Meanwhile 56.1% of campers report being blocked by sold-out sites — up from 10.6% in 2019.
Breaks todayEvery site makes her name a campground first, then checks that one campground alone. "What's open this weekend, anywhere near me" has nowhere to be asked. When she does get shut out, the fallback tier she actually needs — first-come and BLM dispersed — has no reservations, no real-time occupancy, and a discovery model that requires driving to a "FULL" sign.
Would stop herA date picker that opens on today and shows nothing. A results list she has to read campground-by-campground. Anything that takes more than one screen to answer "is this weekend possible."

Key · 02Satisficer"Aspirational Core" · 14%

The Aspirational Beginner

Wants to be outdoorsy. Sticks close to home. Rents rather than commits.

He has done this twice. Both times something went slightly wrong in a way that felt like his fault — the site was gravel, there was no water, the walk to the beach was longer than the listing implied. He's not afraid of the woods. He's afraid of looking like he doesn't belong in them.

Time

Has weekends but spends them deciding. Research time is the cost — planning stress, not trip cost, is what kills the trip.

Money

$400–800 to equip a first trip, all sunk before night one. 28% of campers name gear affordability as a barrier; 22% of first-time campers spend $0 on gear, which means borrowing or renting.

Opportunity

Knowledge, not access. Two-thirds of campers wrongly believe a tent or vehicle is required to claim a first-come site — a false belief suppressing the single most accessible inventory in the country.
Evidence srcThe barrier campers name themselves is money-for-gear, not fear: 28% say they “cannot afford to buy the gear needed for the outdoor experience” — second only to the cost of the trips at 43%. The retention picture is worse than the acquisition one. Only 62% of campers who started camping in the past three years plan to camp again — roughly four in ten recent entrants lapse — while 87% of those who rate their first trip “a great experience” keep camping. The first trip is the whole funnel. On belonging, the strongest figure available is secondary: about a quarter of outdoor participants report feeling unwelcome or out of place, and eight in ten of those are 16–34, which is exactly this persona's cohort.
Breaks todayAbsences go unstated. "No showers, no potable water" is a useful answer; silence is not. A campground with a gravel pad and a 40-minute walk to the water reads identically to one on the shore. Every failure lands as personal incompetence rather than as missing information.
Would stop himVocabulary that assumes he already knows — loop letters, hookup classes, "NR" versus "R". A page that lists what a campground has and stays silent on what it lacks. Anything that makes the first trip feel like an exam.

Key · 03Maximiser by necessityavg. group ≈ 7

The Trip Organizer

One adult carries the whole trip. Usually a parent. Usually a woman.

Four families said yes in the group chat in March. It is now May, two have gone quiet, one has a kid with a nut allergy, and the campground that works for everyone has two sites left on different loops. She has a spreadsheet. Nobody asked her to make a spreadsheet.

Time

19 hours of planning across 11 days and 83 group-chat messages for one trip. Locked to school holidays — June to August, exactly when supply is tightest and priced +20–50%.

Money

Fronts costs and chases reimbursement — Splitwise data shows ~$410/person in shared expenses on a typical group trip. Half of travellers dispute how splitting should even work.

Opportunity

Headcount churn is the real enemy. 76% of group trips never leave the group chat, and 90% of travellers blame the people who won't commit.
Evidence80% of women say they do most of the planning for their group; 62% make most of the decisions. 72% of planners believe the trip would not happen without them, and they are blamed roughly twice as often as anyone admits to blaming them. 71% say they would happily let someone else lead — the demand for an organizer is universal, the willingness to be one is not. 78% of parents find travel planning stressful. 22% of families “camp with grandparents and/or other relatives,” and multigenerational travel touches 28% of campers.
Why yes means nothingEvery number she plans against is derived from a headcount that keeps moving. Free events lose 40–60% of registrants; paid ones hold 70–90%. Even after an explicit yes, roughly one in four does not show — the median confirmed-RSVP-to-attendance rate across 13,800 weddings was 76%. The lever that actually works is a commitment signal at sign-up, which is why deposits exist in organised group travel: they are a headcount-stabilisation mechanism, not a financing one. She has no way to ask for one.
Breaks todayThe plan lives in her head and a screenshot. Sending it means sending a link nobody else can act on, or retyping it. Meanwhile a party of five exceeds the 6–8 person cap on most federal sites, so "one booking" was never an option — the system quietly requires her to hold two reservations and hope they're adjacent.
Would stop herA plan that can't be shared as a link. Any flow that assumes one person, one tent, one decision. Losing her filters and dates when she sends the page to someone else.

04

Two we design around

Real, valuable, and deliberately not the target. Both are named so their constraints stay visible in decisions — not so we build for them now.

Long tail · 04Institutional20–50 people

The Youth-Group Quartermaster

Scout troop, church group, school outdoor-ed. Plans a year out, against rules no consumer software models.

The troop picked its dates at an annual planning conference last September. He needs a site for 34 people, two adults minimum on site at all times, tents arranged so no youth sleeps in an adult's tent, and medical forms for everyone — Part C, signed by a physician, because the trip runs past 72 hours. The campground he wants takes reservations by telephone, weekdays only.

Time

Inverted. Plans 12 months out, not five weeks — Philmont opens two years ahead. But the horizon is not reliably an advantage: group windows are a tendency, not a rule. Capitol Reef releases group sites 12 months out; Bryce's Sunset group site opens just 14 days ahead.

Money

Priced per site, either flat or in headcount bands — Bryce runs $55 for 7–10 people up to $100 for 26–30; Capitol Reef is a flat $125. Enforced minimum occupancy means a shrinking group still pays the block. Cancellation cliff is 14 days for group facilities against 1 day for family sites.

Opportunity

The inventory is off-platform. Texas, Florida, Pennsylvania, Ohio and Michigan all require phone or park-direct booking for group facilities. Only California, New York and Washington put group sites in the same online inventory as family sites.
The dead zoneDifficulty is a step function, not a slope. Average party size on a national forest visit is about 2.4 people — the entire physical system is calibrated for that. A 40-person troop is at once five times that, over the ~8-person line that triggers group-permit paperwork, over the 12-person cap in most designated wilderness (where the group must physically split and keep a mile apart), below the 75-person threshold for a Forest Service group-use permit, and outside the occupancy band of many group sites — Yosemite's take 13–30, others set minimums of 5, 7, 9 or 11. There is no permit path and no product path. Booking six individual sites isn't prohibited, but it collides with per-facility caps of 2–4 reservations per transaction and rules requiring the named person to be present.
The 72-hour cliffA two-night trip needs a signature. A three-night trip needs Part C of the Annual Health and Medical Record — a physician's exam for every participant. For twelve families that is twelve external appointments he does not control, on a deadline. Nothing about the campground changed; the paperwork did.
Rules software ignoresTwo-deep leadership on every outing, and no one-on-one contact including digital. Youth sharing a tent must be within two years of age; no youth sleeps in an adult's tent. Girl Scouts sets ratios by grade and uses the lowest grade in a mixed troop; California requires one adult per 15 youths, Wisconsin one per 10. Drivers need documented insurance — $100,000 combined single limit recommended, $1,000,000 for anything carrying ten or more — with no pickup beds, no pre-2005 15-passenger vans, and a 10-hour daily driving limit. At ~8% childhood food-allergy prevalence, a twelve-family group will almost certainly include a child with a diagnosed allergy, so every shared meal is a constraint problem rather than a multiplication.
What he uses todayNot the official tools. The observed stack is BAND or GroupMe for talk, SignUpGenius for slots, a Google Sheet for the real data, and Cheddar Up or Venmo for money — and nothing joins "signed up" to "paid" to "medical form on file" to "in a car." Scouting America's own Scoutbook is failing at exactly this surface: a troop of 36 found every event showing 24 attendees because sub-unit moves silently dropped Scouts from RSVPs, calendar feeds break when text is pasted from Word, messaging was switched off before its replacement shipped, and the mobile app sits at 2.0 stars across 902 ratings. There is no public API at all, so every adjacent tool is stuck on manual CSV.
Why not nowServing him properly means modelling occupancy minimums, adjacency, per-facility transaction caps, eligibility tests for youth-group pricing — which is a separate SKU, often phone-only, at $15–56/night for 35–50 people against $135–300 for a generic group camp — and an inventory substantially not on the internet. It also means working offline at a trailhead with no signal, which is the one moment the data is life-critical and every cloud tool fails. That is a different product, and the Vision's fifth value — camping-first, deliberately narrow — says so.
And most of it isn't softwareVolunteer leaders arguing this out in the open name three obstacles to going digital: people without email or unwilling to use another form, legal uncertainty about e-signatures on a minor's medical release, and forms sent out too late to be printed before departure. Only the third is a software problem. The settled answer they reach is a hybrid — digital for logistics, printed ink signatures for the medical release — which is why even a well-served troop ends the process at a paper binder. A product that assumes it can absorb the whole workflow has misread the constraint.
What we owe him anywayTreat group-site min/max occupancy and vehicle caps as first-class fields rather than dropping them. They already exist in the source data. Rendering "13–30 people, 5 vehicles" costs nothing and is the difference between a usable page and a misleading one. And never state a group booking rule globally — almost nothing here is set centrally, so a confident "group sites open 12 months out" will be wrong often enough to cost trust.
Two things worth knowing before anyone argues to build for him. First, the incumbent has said publicly that it will not — Scouting America's own volunteer support answers feature requests with "there is no money to rewrite it" and "significant staff reductions due to bankruptcy, including IT," and points leaders at GroupMe instead. That is simultaneously the size of the opening and the reason it stays open. Second, and more useful to us: offline is both unmet and unrequested. Search their forum for working without signal and there is nothing — volunteers stopped asking and quietly reverted to paper ("paper is portable and the batteries never die"). A need with no demand signal will never surface in research, and will still decide whether a product survives contact with a trailhead.
Long tail · 05Maximiser~5% core segment

The Site Loyalist

Wants site 43 at Blackwoods, second week of July. Already has tools for this.

She knows which loop is quietest, which sites back onto the trail, and what time the reservation window opens in her own timezone. She does not need to be told what Acadia is like. She needs to be told, within seconds, that something opened.

Time

Will set an alarm for a 7am release six months out. Time is not a constraint; it is a weapon she already uses.

Money

Willing to pay — an entire paid industry exists on this, from $9 single scans to $75/year memberships.

Opportunity

Already solved by incumbents. Campflare scans every campground every ~45 seconds, free, and has sent 9M+ notifications.
Why long tailCore participants are ~5% of outdoor consumers, and this need is the most competitively served in the entire category. Building for her means competing with a free 45-second scanner on latency alone — a race decided by infrastructure spend, not design.
Why she still mattersOur alerting backend already does everything hers needs — multi-target watches, per-watch cadence to 5 seconds, delivery — behind a form whose first field is a numeric POI ID. Slice 4 serves her for almost nothing, and the free + in-context + multi-campground combination is one nobody ships. That is worth taking, as long as it is not mistaken for the strategy.

What the grid says

  • Nobody runs fewer than four apps, and no app spans more than two stages. Every stack is a chain of handoffs held together by a person retyping. That is the actual competitive landscape — not any one rival, but the seams between them.
  • Booking is the only stage with a monopoly, and it is the only stage nobody attacks. Recreation.gov sits in four of five columns and every competitor in the category — The Dyrt, Campnab, Campflare, Hipcamp's public inventory — ends by handing the user to it. Deciding not to be a booking engine is not a limitation, it is the category's standing consensus. Our job is to be the best thing that hands off to it.
  • Camping software owns Discover and has disappeared by Coordinate. In the Discover row, four of five columns open a purpose-built outdoor product. In the Coordinate row, none do — every column has switched to a chat app, a spreadsheet, a sign-up page and a payments app. The category stops at the moment a trip becomes other people's business, and hands the rest to general-purpose software that knows nothing about campsites.
  • The stack grows with group size, and it grows in the wrong direction. One person needs three apps; the organizer needs eight; the quartermaster needs nine and still ends at paper. Each added person adds a tool that knows nothing about the campsite.
  • Two rows are empty for the people we most want. The Weekender and the Beginner have nothing in the watch row — one abandons, the other doesn't know alerting exists. That is the single clearest product opening on this page, and Slice 4 is already most of the way to filling it.
  • Every column degrades to screenshots or paper at the trailhead. Even the Loyalist. Signal is where all five stacks fail identically, and where nobody is competing.
The pattern in one line: the category sells inspiration and sells latency, and leaves the middle — deciding, agreeing, and carrying the decision — to a group chat. That middle is exactly what a shareable URL carrying dates, filters and availability addresses, which is why the smallest item in M1a is also the most strategically loaded one. We do not need to replace anything in this grid. We need to be the one artifact that survives being pasted into the chat app in row five.
Sourcing, since it was asked. The Quartermaster column is the only one quote-backed — BAND/GroupMe, SignUpGenius, Google Sheets, Cheddar Up and the 2.0-star Scoutbook app all come from volunteers describing their own stacks in Scouting America's public forum, plus the incumbent's own support replies. Splitwise, Campnab, Campflare, The Dyrt, Hipcamp and Recreation.gov are documented products with published behaviour. The rest of the grid is inference — a plausible reconstruction from the barrier and behaviour data in Sources, not observed usage. Confirming or breaking it is precisely what the five interviews are for, and it is a cheap thing to ask: show me every app you opened for your last trip, in order.
06

Why groups break every planning tool

Families and youth organisations share a shape that consumer software consistently fails to anticipate. It is worth stating separately, because it explains both persona 3 and persona 4.

  • The system is built for a party of two. Average group size on a national forest visit is about 2.4 people, and site sizing, vehicle pads, per-site fees and the reservation system are all calibrated to it. Federal sites commonly cap at 6–8; Sequoia and Yellowstone at 6 or "one family."
  • Difficulty is a step function, not a slope. A three-family trip of twelve is simultaneously five times the design party size, over the ~8-person line that triggers group-permit paperwork, at or over the 12-person wilderness cap that forces a physical split, and in need of either three sites or one scarce group site with an entirely separate booking process. Nothing about that is gradual.
  • Group inventory is a different product with different rules. Minimum and maximum occupancy, vehicle caps, banded pricing by headcount, a 14-day cancellation cliff against 1 day — and in five of eight states surveyed, no online booking at all. Booking windows vary from 14 days to 12 months within the same agency, so almost nothing here can be stated globally without being wrong somewhere.
  • Headcount is not a number, it's a moving target — and nobody can stabilise it. Free events lose 40–60% of registrants; even a confirmed yes converts to attendance only about three times in four. Minimums are enforced, so a group that shrinks still pays the block. The one lever that demonstrably works is a commitment signal at sign-up, which is why organised group travel runs on deposits — they are a headcount-stabilisation mechanism, not a financing one. An informal organizer has no contract, no penalty, and a friendship with every defector.
  • The binding constraints are non-spatial. Adult ratios, sleeping arrangements, driver insurance limits, medical forms with a 72-hour cliff, allergy management at ~8% childhood prevalence. No amount of map, drive time or availability data touches any of it, and a tool that assumes the hard part is finding a campsite is answering the wrong question.
  • Nobody has one tool; everybody has four. Chat, a sign-up sheet, a spreadsheet, and a payment app — with nothing joining "signed up" to "paid" to "form on file" to "in a car." The free tiers are squeezed exactly where trips get real: one active poll, five items per collection page, custom questions behind a paywall. And the official tools fail hardest at the trip surface, which is why the consumer ones win on attention while the official ones keep the record.
  • The organizer is a single point of failure with no leverage. One person, 19 hours, 83 messages, fronting the money, blamed twice as often as anyone admits. The most valuable thing a planning product can give this person is not a better search — it is a link that carries the whole decision, so the work becomes reviewable instead of re-explainable.
What this justifies in M1a. Exactly one thing, and it is already scoped: dates and filters in the URL, so a destination plus a window is one shareable link. That is the entire group feature set we should build now. The trip object (M1b) is where the rest belongs, and the youth-org rules belong nowhere until the product is deliberately widened.

Two barriers are ours, and they are the two the roadmap already points at. The six-month booking window and the knowledge gap are the only structural constraints a planning product can meaningfully move — and they map exactly onto the date window and onto stating what a campground lacks. Everything else on this table is real, is larger, and is not a software problem. Say so rather than implying otherwise.
08

What this changes

  • The Vision's six collapse into three. First-timers and off-season travellers are both the Aspirational Beginner meeting different failures. Families and the determined planner separate cleanly into an organizer problem and a latency problem. RV and trailer travellers turn out to be a filter — rig length, hookups, clearance — exactly as value 1 already argued, not a persona.
  • Two of three key personas are satisficers, and the entire category is built for maximisers. That is the strongest argument yet for the weekend shelf and for the date window leading the product rather than following a campground choice.
  • Retention beats acquisition. 87% of great first experiences convert and only 43% are great. Every honesty rule in the Vision — stamp the freshness, closed is not full, state the absences, never overclaim — is retention machinery, not politeness.
  • Group support is one URL, not a feature area. Dates and filters in the link serves the organizer today; everything past that is M1b or out of scope.
  • Two numbers were withdrawn under checking. See Sources — the belonging and first-timer-intake figures did not survive a trace to a primary source, and the claims that rested on them have been rewritten around evidence that did.
  • The gap that would change the most is still five interviews. Specifically: watch one person plan a weekend from scratch, and one organizer coordinate four families. Everything on this page is inference over other people's surveys.

Two figures failed the trace and are gone from the argument. “79% of young adults wish they had more outdoor skills” and “38% report feeling unwelcome in the outdoor community” could not be tied to any published instrument — no report, sample, or year.
