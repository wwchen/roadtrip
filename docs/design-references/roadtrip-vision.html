Roadtrip Roadtrip Vision 15 Aug 2026

The point of view

Planning a camping trip today takes twenty browser tabs. A reservation site that will not tell you what is nearby. A park site that will not tell you what is open. A maps tab per campground, and a forum thread from 2019. Roadtrip collapses all of it into one place that knows the terrain — the easiest way to plan a trip, book it, and send it to the people coming with you. This document holds the point of view. The milestones live in their own documents.

1 · The problem

Planning the trip should be the good part

Nobody else puts the whole picture in one place. That's the opening.

A national park is a world of its own, and looking forward to it is half the trip.

  • Somebody planning Acadia is picturing a place, not a coordinate. Which campgrounds sit near what they came for. How long the drive is. Whether the site takes a trailer.
  • Today each of those answers lives on a different website. Assembling them by hand is the tedious part of a trip that should be the fun part.
  • Confidence comes from context, not from more results. Booking.com curates a city; we curate a park.
  • Availability says whether you can book. Features and distance say whether you should. We ship only the first half today.
  • A campground with no potable water is a useful answer, as long as we say so.
  • Hierarchy is the mechanism. Three page types, rendered the same way every time, linked to each other. That is what turns a vague "Arcadia" into Acadia National Park.
2 · The bet

A map enriched until it is a database you can look at

Why this is a spatial problem, and why that is not a chat.

Planning a camping trip is spatial reasoning, and spatial reasoning does not fit in a chat.

  • An assistant can tell you Kalaloch is two hours from Hurricane Ridge. It cannot hold six campgrounds side by side while you change your mind three times.
  • The response is to enrich the map until it is a database you can look at. Every pin carries kind, cost, season, availability for your nights and drive time from where you'd sleep.
  • Filters do the narrowing. Rig length, hookups, showers, walkable to water, first-come in or out. They act on the map and the list together.
  • Google Maps will route you to a campground and say nothing about whether you'd want to be there in June. Temperature at elevation, road and water turn-on, winter closures — those belong on the canvas.
  • Everyone else drops a layer. Maps have geography and no availability. Reservation sites have availability and no geography. Assistants describe both and show neither.
  • Our availability polling is the moat. Right now it renders as a dot.
3 · Values

Six values, each one visible in something we gave up

A value nobody paid for is a slogan. Every value below is stated with the thing it cost us, taken from decisions already made in the reframe deck and the reskin proposal.

1 · Serve the trip, not the vehicleCost: EV as the core

Roadtrip started as EV-aware routing. Most road trippers do not drive electric, and EV-only caps the market before anything else is proven. Range and chargers survive as constraints a trip can carry. They stopped being the product.

Reads asRig length, hookups and clearance are filters. No vehicle type gets its own surface.

2 · Dates are the constraint nobody plans aroundCost: place-first search

The question is not "where is a dark campsite." It is "which campsite, on which specific nights." New moons are fixed years out, permits have hard release dates, an eclipse can 10× lodging overnight. Every one of those starts from a date you do not control.

Reads asOne date window per trip, set once, inherited by every surface and carried in the URL.

3 · An integration problem, not a content problemCost: becoming a guidebook

The defensible thing is joining live availability to distance, season and physical fit. Prose is cheap to copy and expensive to keep true. The reframe deck named its own kill criteria: if a competitor ships the filters and the calendar, the edge is gone.

Reads asStructured resolution is a Must. Hand-written destination copy is a Nice, capped at one line per campground.

4 · Data language is sacred, chrome is negotiableCost: a tidier palette

Layer hues and the availability vocabulary carry meaning, so they are not style. The rule from the reskin proposal: if a colour identifies data it belongs to Roadtrip; if it paints chrome it comes from the design system. A red→green signal ramp was cut for colliding with availability.

Reads asM0 repaints chrome and is forbidden from touching the data vocabulary.

5 · Camping-first, deliberately narrowCost: general trip planning

Two planning shapes exist in this category: people alignment, and route plus physical constraints. Trying to serve both is how Roadtrippers ended up spread thin. Staying camping-first keeps the constraint problem specific enough to actually build.

Reads asNo city trips, no generic itineraries, no gear marketplace, no community reviews.

6 · Trust breaks in this category, so it is ours to winCost: confident-looking numbers

Review mining found one pattern across three apps: free tiers that feel like demos, collaboration that is half-solved, and failures that land exactly while somebody is planning. Trust is the category's soft spot.

Reads asFreshness stamps, closed is never rendered as full, no forecast past ten days, and no number we cannot observe.

4 · Pillars

Three pillars the values roll up into

Values settle arguments. Pillars decide what gets built.

The constraint engine

The moat

Join date, live availability, drive time and physical fit in one query. Nobody else holds all four. This is the thing that is hard to copy and expensive to maintain, which is exactly why it is worth owning.

One canvas

The differentiator

Geography, time and conditions legible at once, outdoors, in sunlight. Comparing six campgrounds is a spatial act. A list cannot do it and a chat cannot do it.

Earned trust

The wedge

Say what we know, say when we knew it, and never overclaim. In a category where the incumbents fail at the moment of planning, being reliably honest is a feature.

What is missing, stated plainly.

  • No user interviews. Every segment above is desk research. The six campers in the next section are inferred, not observed, and nobody has watched a real person plan a trip.
  • No usage data. The running app has real users behind auth, and we do not look at what they do.
  • No scarcity data. We are not retaining availability history, so we cannot measure how fast openings vanish — the fact that would prove or kill the alerting thesis.

6 · Influence

How the values pick the milestones

Each milestone exists because of a value, and each one is bounded by a different value. That is the test for whether a piece of work belongs.

Milestone Exists because of Bounded by What that rules out
M0 Value 4. Chrome is negotiable, so it can be swapped wholesale and cheaply. Value 4 again. Data language is sacred, so the availability vocabulary and layer hues are untouchable. Any change to the status words, the layer colours, or an endpoint. That is the whole scope rule.
M1a Values 2 and 3. Dates organise the product; joined data is the edge. Value 6. Nothing ships that we cannot state honestly. Chances of getting in, until history exists. Watcher counts, permanently. A temperature we cannot source.
M2 Values 2 and 3, finally joined. Hyper search is the constraint query the strategy was built on. Pillar 2. The canvas stays the place you choose; the assistant narrows and explains. Chat as the primary surface. Any answer the canvas cannot show.
M3 Value 5. Discovery across destinations, still camping-first. Value 1. Serve the trip, so no vehicle-specific product. City trips, generic itineraries, EV-only routing as a headline.

Where the constraint query lives — decided

M1a is destination-first on purpose. The constraint query moves to M2, powered by the assistant.

  • The strategy was built on one query: "campsites within four hours, on new-moon weekends, currently bookable." Joining drive time, a date rule nobody controls, and live availability.
  • That question fits language and tools far better than it fits a form. Six filters and a calendar cannot express "new-moon weekends" without a bespoke control per constraint, and the next constraint needs another one.
  • M1a earns the right to ask it. The destination index, the availability grains, the drive times and the normalised amenities are exactly what the query joins.
  • It lands in M2 as hyper search, where the assistant parses the sentence, the verbs do the work, and the canvas shows the answer.

The thing to hold on to: the moat is the join, not the chat. If hyper search never arrives, we have built a nicer Recreation.gov. It is now a named section in M2 rather than an ambition.

The decision still open from the reframe

Are we building this for ourselves, or for customers?

  • Direction A — personal project. Route, campsites and a shared plan link. Success is using it on a real trip and closing the other tabs. Near-zero cost, honest scope.
  • Direction B — product with a moat. Constraint-first trip search as an integration problem. Metered API access or a per-trip unlock. Kill criteria already written: if a competitor ships the filters and the permit calendar, it is over.
  • Everything on this page holds under either. What changes is what "done" means, how much index coverage M1 needs, and whether the missing research becomes urgent.

This was left open in the reframe deck and is still open. It is the only question on this page that blocks nothing today and changes everything later.

7 · Users

Six kinds of camper, six different first questions

What separates them is which fact they need first — and today that fact is the one thing no site puts on the page.

Superseded, and worth reading against. These six were inferred, as this page said at the time. They have since been replaced by three evidence-grounded personas and two we design around, each anchored to published participation and barrier data. Three of the six survived, two merged and one turned out to be a filter rather than a person — see what this changes. The mapping, card by card: families and big groups became the Trip Organizer; first-timers and off-season travellers became the Aspirational Beginner; the determined planner split into the Flexible Weekender and the Site Loyalist; and RV and trailer travellers turned out to be a set of filters, exactly as value 1 already argued.

Families with young kids

Into: a beach or lake they can walk to, ranger programs, short drives, being able to bail out early.

  • Flush toilets, showers, potable water
  • Drive-up, not walk-in; room for two tents
  • Near a restroom, but not next to one
  • Under ~20 min to the thing the kids came for
👩"Is there stroller space? Somewhere to change a baby? What is actually good to do with kids here? My mother is coming too, so how far is the walk from the car?"

Breaks today. A site shows as available with no hint it's forty minutes from the water, and "no showers" is left out rather than stated.

RV and trailer travellers

Into: hookups, pull-through sites, and a road they can actually get down.

  • Max vehicle length as a hard filter
  • Electric / water / sewer, dump station
  • Generator hours, back-in vs pull-through
  • Grade and low-clearance warnings on the way in
🚐"Will a 32-foot trailer fit down that road, and is there a dump station when I get there?"

Breaks today. Length limits sit in per-site fine print. Booking a 32 ft trailer into a 21 ft loop is an expensive, entirely preventable mistake.

First-timers at a big park

Into: seeing the famous things, and not spending the trip in the car.

  • The shape of the park — which zones exist
  • What a day looks like from each campground
  • Which side to sleep on, given what they want to see
🧭"I have four days. Which side of the park do I even sleep on?"

Breaks today. Nothing teaches geography. Olympic's Kalaloch and Hurricane Ridge are a couple of hours apart and no site says so before you've booked.

The flexible weekender

Into: getting out at all. Where is negotiable; when is not.

  • Search by dates, not by campground name
  • Anything open within a few hours of home
  • First-come sites treated as real options
🤔"I did not bring anything on this trip, so I have almost no camping gear. How cold does it get? Do I need hiking shoes for Zion National Park?"

Breaks today. Every site makes you name a campground first, then checks it alone. The question "what's open this weekend" has nowhere to be asked.

The determined planner

Into: one specific campground, often one specific loop, booked six months out.

  • Alerts across several campgrounds and a date range
  • Fast, honest notification — openings vanish
  • A sense of whether waiting is worth it
⏰"Site 43 at Blackwoods, second week of July. Tell me the second it opens."

Breaks today. This is campflare's whole product, and ours already works — behind a form whose first field is a numeric POI ID.

Off-season travellers

Into: empty trails, cheaper nights, and the shoulder weeks nobody else wants.

  • What is genuinely open this month
  • Water shut off, roads gated, loops closed
  • Which months go first-come-only
🍂"It says no availability. Is it full, or is it shut until May?"

Breaks today. "No availability" and "closed until May" render identically. A closed campground reads as a full one, and the trip gets cancelled for no reason.

Why this beats what they use now

Four tools, four ends of the same trip, and none of them covers the middle — which is where all six of the people above actually spend their time.

Google Maps

A great map, a clunky planner
  • Pro. Unbeatable at geography and at getting you there.
  • Con. No availability, no season, no sense that a campground shuts in October.
  • Result. One browser tab per candidate.

Recreation.gov

A checkout counter
  • Pro. The real inventory, and the only place a booking completes.
  • Con. Organised by facility, not by place. Unavailable hides whether it is full or closed.
  • Result. It finishes a decision. It does not help you make one.

Campflare

An alarm clock
  • Pro. Watches a campground and tells you the moment it opens.
  • Con. No geography, no browsing, no reason to open it before you know what you want.
  • Result. It serves the last five minutes of planning.

Asking an AI

Fluent, unverifiable, one answer at a time
  • Pro. Good at describing a park and narrowing a vague idea.
  • Con. Blind to live inventory, and hands you one answer where the choice needs six.
  • Result. Our own assistant lands in M2, on top of this canvas rather than instead of it.

Roadtrip

The canvas in between
  • Pro. One surface carrying geography, drive time, season and availability at once.
  • Pro. Filters narrow the map and the list together, for your dates.
  • Con. We don't take the booking, so the hand-off has to be clean and honest.

8 · Trust

What earns trust

We are asking people to plan a trip on our reading of someone else's data. These rules make that reading credible, and each is testable in review.

Say what we know

  • Stamp the freshness. Availability without a last-checked time is a guess wearing a number's clothes.
  • Closed is not full. A campground shut for the season and a campground with no space are different answers and must never render alike.
  • State the absences. "No showers, no potable water" is a useful answer. Silence is not.
  • Name the operator at the point of hand-off, so nobody is surprised by where they land.

Never overclaim

  • A drive time is a promise. If it is straight-line rather than routed, the label says so.
  • No forecast past ten days. People book six months out; a confident October temperature costs more trust than a blank.
  • No numbers we cannot observe. Watcher counts and watch-to-booking conversion are out permanently — booking happens off-site.
  • No silent dead ends. Every "nothing here" carries the nearest thing that does work.

10 · Scope

What we are not building

Goals

  • Understand the destination. A park page should convey geography — what is where, and how far apart, in minutes rather than miles. Distance is the fact that makes every other fact usable.
  • Book with confidence. Campground facts normalised across operators, absences included. Availability answers whether you can; features and drive time answer whether you should.
  • Uniform, linkable pages. Every campground, park and place rendered the same way, pointing at each other. Hierarchy is what resolves a vague "Arcadia" into a rich destination.
  • Dates once, everywhere. Tell us when you're free and we answer for the whole destination — instead of asking again on every campground page.
  • Shareable by default. A destination plus a date window is one URL. Sending the plan to the people coming with you should not require a screenshot.
  • M0 first. Put the design system on every existing screen before new structure lands, so all of the above gets built once and the theme is de-risked on real screens.
  • Build the foundation, not the demo. The page types, the date model and the URL contract are chosen so planning, booking and sharing extend from them later. Nothing here should need retrofitting.

Non-goals

  • Not a booking engine. Reservations happen on the operator's site. We are the surface that decides what to book; we hand off to complete it.
  • Not in M0: any change to information architecture, behaviour, or endpoints — including search relevance.
  • Not in M1a: the trip object — origin, ordered stays, return leg, "leave by". That is M1b and needs its own design pass.
  • Not now: multi-destination itineraries, EV range and trailer routing, city trips with no camping hook.
  • Not ever, as specified: watcher counts and watch-to-booking conversion. Booking happens off-site; we cannot observe it.

One of four. Vision, M0, M1 and M2 are separate documents on purpose — disparate work with different readers, not stages of one feature.
